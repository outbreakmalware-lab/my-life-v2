I built entire worlds nobody was ever going to see.

Years inside Minecraft. Not playing — building. Cities with transit that actually worked. Redstone contraptions held together with hope. Then shader packs, because the world looked wrong and I wanted to know why light behaved like that.

That's the whole origin story: something didn't feel right, so I opened it up. Nobody asked me to. Nobody was watching. It turns out that's the only reason I've ever learned anything.

Every good thing I've made stands on a pile of genuinely terrible code.

Websites that hurt to look at. Bots that DM'd 400 people by accident. A shader that turned the sky into a strobe light and gave my friend a headache. Nested callbacks so deep they had their own weather.

I kept every one of them. Not out of nostalgia — out of evidence. Broken things teach faster and remember longer than the ones that work on the first try.

asmodeus@localhost:~/humans$ cat principles.txt

I'd rather be the person you can message at 2am than the person with the best repo.

Code is the fun part. But everything I've made worth keeping came out of someone else needing it — a friend, a server, a person who couldn't find a tool so I wrote one badly and then wrote it again properly.

How I help

Actually reads the error

Send me your stack trace. I'll read the whole thing, not just the first line, and I won't make you feel stupid for asking. Everyone was googling "what is undefined" once.

How I work

Boring code, loud ideas

Be adventurous with what you build, conservative with how you build it. Clever code is a letter to your future self written in a language you'll have forgotten.

What I believe

Credit is free

Say who helped. Link the tutorial. Name the person whose snippet you adapted. It costs nothing and it's how the whole thing keeps working.

The rule

Don't punch down

No dunking on beginners, no "just" in explanations, no gatekeeping a hobby that's supposed to be joyful. If you know something, hand it over.
